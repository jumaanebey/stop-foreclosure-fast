The California Homeowner Bill of Rights, in plain English

If you're facing foreclosure in California, you have more legal protection than homeowners in almost any other state. Here's what those protections actually are — and how to use them.

A quick word about me before we start: I'm not an attorney, and this isn't legal advice. I'm one person who helps California homeowners understand where they stand, get organized, and — when the situation calls for it — get connected with an attorney or a free HUD-approved counselor. What follows is the education I wish every homeowner had before their first missed payment.

What the Homeowner Bill of Rights is

The Homeowner Bill of Rights (HBOR) is a set of California laws — primarily Civil Code sections 2923.4 through 2924.12 — passed in 2012 after the foreclosure abuses of the housing crisis, with its core protections made permanent in 2018. It doesn't prevent foreclosure. What it does is force servicers to follow a fair process, and it gives you real remedies when they don't.

Your major protections

1. A single point of contact (Civil Code § 2923.7)

Once you ask your servicer about foreclosure alternatives, they must assign you a single person or team responsible for your file. That contact must know your case, be able to tell you where your application stands, and have access to people with authority to make decisions.

Before this law, homeowners got bounced between representatives who each said something different. Now you can insist on one contact — ask for their direct information and write it down.

2. The dual-tracking ban (Civil Code § 2923.6)

This is your most powerful protection. While your servicer is reviewing a complete application for a loan modification, they cannot simultaneously push the foreclosure forward. Specifically, they may not:

  • Record a Notice of Default while your complete first-lien modification application is under review
  • Record a Notice of Trustee's Sale during the review
  • Conduct the foreclosure sale while the review, or a timely appeal, is pending
  • Foreclose while you're performing on a trial payment plan

In practice: if you submit a complete modification application on Monday and a sale is set for Friday, the sale has to wait until the review is finished and any appeal period has run.

The word "complete" carries all the weight

Dual-tracking protection only kicks in when your application is complete — every document your servicer requires, submitted. One missing bank statement and the protection doesn't attach. Submit in writing, keep copies of everything, and get proof of delivery (a portal confirmation or certified mail). I help homeowners assemble and double-check these packages so nothing is missing.

3. A real review, and a written answer

Your servicer must actually evaluate you for the alternatives they offer — not just go through the motions. If they deny your first-lien modification application, they must tell you why in writing, and you generally have 30 days to appeal with additional documentation. A denial letter that gives no reasons is itself a red flag.

4. Contact before the foreclosure even starts (Civil Code § 2923.5)

Before recording a Notice of Default, your servicer must contact you — or make diligent, documented attempts to — to discuss your finances and alternatives, and then wait at least 30 days. On top of that, federal servicing rules generally prohibit starting foreclosure until you're more than 120 days delinquent.

5. Strict notice timing

StepMinimum timing
First missed payment → Notice of DefaultGenerally 120+ days delinquent (federal rule)
Notice of Default → Notice of Trustee's SaleAt least 90 days
Notice of Trustee's Sale → auction dateAt least 21 days

What violations look like

  • Dual tracking: a sale date moving forward while your complete application sits "in review"
  • Lost paperwork: repeatedly being told documents were never received
  • Inconsistent answers: different representatives telling you different things about the same file
  • Improper notices: wrong timing, missing information, or notices you never received
  • Robo-signing: foreclosure documents signed without anyone actually reviewing them
  • Fee padding: charges on your account no one can explain

How to enforce your rights

Document everything

  • Keep every letter, email, and notice — including the envelope postmarks
  • Log every phone call: date, time, name of the representative, what was said
  • Photograph anything posted on your property
  • Keep proof of when you submitted each document

Report violations

  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — file a complaint at consumerfinance.gov; servicers must respond
  • California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation — dfpi.ca.gov
  • California Attorney General — oag.ca.gov for systematic abuse
  • An attorney — for serious violations, especially with a sale date approaching

What enforcement can win

Under Civil Code § 2924.12, a material HBOR violation can support a court order stopping the sale before it happens, and damages after it does — with attorney's fees available to homeowners who win injunctive relief. That fee provision matters: it means attorneys will often take a strong HBOR case even when the homeowner has no money for legal fees. If you think your rights were violated, that's the moment to talk to a lawyer — and if you don't know one, I'll help you find one.

The honest limitations

HBOR guarantees a fair process, not a saved home. The protections mostly apply to owner-occupied homes of one to four units on first mortgages — investment and commercial properties get less. And none of it helps if you don't engage: if you ignore the servicer's letters and never submit an application, there's no review for the law to protect.
